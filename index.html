Part A

A1The setting

What it means for random variables to "converge".

A random variable is a function X: Ω → ℝ on a probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P). For a sequence of numbers there is only one natural notion of convergence, but a sequence of random variables X1, X2, … is a sequence of functions, and "Xn gets close to X" can mean different things:

  • for (almost) every single outcome ω, the numbers Xn(ω) converge (a path-by-path statement);
  • for large n, the probability of being far from X is small (a statement about one n at a time);
  • the average squared distance from X goes to zero;
  • only the distributions get close, the variables themselves can stay far apart (they don't even need to live on the same Ω).

In the previous homeworks I already used the LLN and the CLT with simulations of hackers and systems: here I try to be precise about what kind of convergence each theorem actually gives.

A2The four modes

Definitions, from the strongest "path" notion to the weakest.

Almost sure (a.s.)

P( { ω : Xn(ω) → X(ω) } ) = 1

The sequence of numbers Xn(ω) converges for every ω outside a set of probability 0. Equivalent form: for every ε > 0, P( supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε ) → 0, i.e. eventually the path stays inside the ε-band forever.

In probability (P)

∀ε > 0:   P( |Xn − X| > ε ) → 0

For each fixed large n, it's unlikely that Xn is far from X. It says nothing about what one path does for different n: a path can still leave the band again and again, as long as it happens at different times for different ω.

In mean square (L²)

E[ (Xn − X)² ] → 0

The mean squared error goes to 0 (in general Lp: E|Xn − X|p → 0). It controls the size of the deviations, not only how often they happen: a rare but huge deviation can kill it. It's the natural mode for estimators, since MSE = variance + bias².

In distribution (D)

FXn(x) → FX(x)   at every x where FX is continuous

Only the CDFs converge. Xn and X don't have to be close as random variables (or even be defined on the same space). Equivalent: E[g(Xn)] → E[g(X)] for every bounded continuous g, or convergence of the characteristic functions (Lévy).

A3How they are related

Which mode implies which, and counterexamples for the arrows that don't exist.

implication that always holdspartial converse

The implications

  • a.s. ⇒ P. If the path eventually stays in the ε-band forever, in particular it is in the band at time n with probability → 1 (P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ P(supk≥n|Xk − X| > ε) → 0).
  • L² ⇒ P. By Chebyshev (Markov on the square): P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xn − X)²]/ε² → 0. Same for any Lp, and L² ⇒ L¹.
  • P ⇒ D. If Xn is close to X with high probability, their CDFs are close at continuity points.
  • Partial converses: D ⇒ P when the limit is a constant c (then P(|Xn − c| > ε) = Fn(c − ε) + 1 − Fn(c + ε) → 0); P ⇒ a.s. along some subsequence (Riesz); P ⇒ L² if the Xn² are uniformly integrable (e.g. bounded).

The arrows that don't exist (counterexamples)

I simulated each one with 20,000 paths up to n = 2,000 (ε = 0.5). The last column estimates P(supn≤k≤2000|Xk| > ε): if it doesn't go to 0, the paths keep leaving the band, so there is no a.s. convergence.

  • P but not a.s.: independent Xn ~ Bernoulli(1/n). P(Xn = 1) = 1/n → 0, but since Σ1/n = ∞ and the events are independent, the second Borel-Cantelli lemma says that Xn = 1 happens infinitely often with probability 1. The simulated "tail" probability is still 0.50 at n = 1,000, and with an infinite horizon it would be exactly 1 (P(no 1 between n and N) = (n − 1)/N → 0 as N → ∞). With Bernoulli(1/n²) instead, Σ1/n² < ∞ and the first Borel-Cantelli lemma gives a.s. convergence: the tail probability drops like 1/n.
  • P and L² but not a.s.: the "typewriter". Xn is the indicator of an interval of length 1/k that slides across [0, 1] (k = 1, 2, 3, …, each block sweeping the whole interval). P(Xn = 1) = 1/k → 0, but every ω is hit once in every block: in my simulation each ω had Xn = 1 between 62 and 63 times for n ≤ 2,000. So Xn(ω) converges for no ω at all.
  • a.s. but not L²: Xn = n·1{U < 1/n}. For every U > 0, Xn = 0 as soon as n > 1/U, so it goes to 0 a.s. But E[Xn²] = n²·(1/n) = n → ∞ (simulated: 1,050 at n = 1,000). The rare spikes get bigger and bigger: they are too rare to matter for a.s. or P, but they dominate the mean square.
  • D but not P: Xn = −Z with Z ~ N(0, 1). Since −Z has the same distribution as Z, Xn → Z in distribution trivially. But |Xn − Z| = 2|Z|, so P(|Xn − Z| > 0.5) = P(|Z| > 0.25) = 0.80 for every n (simulated 0.804).

A5The Law of Large Numbers

The same statement in three modes.

Let X1, X2, … be i.i.d. with mean μ, and X̄n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n.

VersionStatementAssumptionsProof idea
L² LLNE[(X̄n − μ)²] = σ²/n → 0uncorrelated, finite variance σ²one line: variance of a sum of uncorrelated variables
Weak LLNX̄n → μ in probabilitywith Chebyshev: finite variance; in general (Khinchin): only E|X| < ∞L² ⇒ P with Chebyshev: P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²)
Strong LLNX̄n → μ almost surelyi.i.d. and E|X| < ∞ (Kolmogorov; Etemadi: pairwise independent is enough)harder: truncation + Borel-Cantelli, or the easy version with E[X⁴] < ∞

Why the difference matters. The weak LLN says that, for a given large n, the sample mean is probably close to μ. The strong LLN says that, with probability 1, the whole sequence of sample means of a single experiment converges, i.e. if I keep collecting data the running average eventually stays close to μ forever. This is what justifies interpreting probability as long-run frequency, and what lets a single long simulation estimate a mean (Monte Carlo).

Simulation with X ~ Uniform(0, 1), 5,000 independent runs, ε = 0.02:

All three criteria go to 0, and the mean squared error matches σ²/n = 1/(12n) exactly. The tail probability (a.s. criterion) is always larger than the single-n one, as it should be: at n = 1,000 only 2.6% of the runs are outside the band, but 5.5% will still leave it at some later time before 10,000. Chebyshev's bound is correct but very loose (0.21 instead of 0.026).

A6The Central Limit Theorem

Only convergence in distribution, and it can't be more.

For i.i.d. variables with mean μ and finite variance σ² > 0:

Zn = √n (X̄n − μ)/σ → N(0, 1)   in distribution

This is the only mode in which the CLT holds. Zn doesn't converge in probability (and so not a.s. or in L²) to any random variable: if it did, Zn and Z2n would have to get close. But since S2n = Sn + (the next n terms), and the two parts are independent,

Cov(Zn, Z2n) = Cov(Sn, S2n)/(σ²√(n·2n)) = nσ²/(σ²n√2) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707   for every n

so Zn − Z2n has variance 2 − √2 ≈ 0.59 that doesn't go to 0. Each new block of data contributes "fresh" randomness of the same size as the old one.

Try it
Zn (x) vs Z2n (y), 2,000 runs
histogram of Zn and N(0,1)

The distribution of Zn is already almost exactly N(0,1), but the cloud of (Zn, Z2n) never collapses onto the diagonal, whatever n. A sharper way to see that there is no a.s. limit is the law of the iterated logarithm: limsup Zn/√(2 log log n) = 1 a.s., so every single path of Zn keeps oscillating and goes beyond any fixed level infinitely often.

A8Why √n?

It all comes from the additivity of variance.

For any random variables with finite variance,

Var(Sn) = Σi Var(Xi) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj)

If the Xi are independent (actually uncorrelated is enough), all the covariances vanish and the variances simply add: Var(Sn) = nσ². So the standard deviation of the sum grows like σ√n, not like n: the random errors partly cancel. As a consequence:

  • Sn − nμ is of order √n. Dividing by n (the mean) gives fluctuations of order 1/√n → 0: that's the LLN.
  • Dividing by √n gives fluctuations of order 1, neither vanishing nor exploding: that's the only scale where a non-trivial limit distribution can appear, and the CLT says that it's normal.
  • Dividing by nα with α < ½ the variance n1−2ασ² blows up; with α > ½ it goes to 0 (and the limit is the constant 0).

Consequences in statistical inference

  • Standard error. SE(X̄) = σ/√n. Confidence intervals X̄ ± 1.96·s/√n shrink like 1/√n.
  • Sample size. To halve the error it takes 4 times the data; to gain one more decimal digit, 100 times. In Homework 4, the spread of the hackers' sampling means went from 0.504 at n = 10 to 0.051 at n = 1,000.
  • Monte Carlo. The error of a simulation estimate is σ/√N whatever the dimension of the problem. That's why simulations get accurate slowly but steadily.
  • Tests. Test statistics are built as (estimate − value under H0)/(SE ∝ 1/√n). With a fixed true difference, the statistic grows like √n, which is why with huge samples tiny effects become "significant" (Homework 6).

A9The role of independence (and of the variance)

What happens when the conditions fail.

Positive correlation. If the observations are correlated the covariance terms don't vanish. For a stationary AR(1) process with lag-1 correlation ρ (Var X = 1), n·Var(X̄n) → (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) instead of 1. The rate is still √n, but the constant changes, and treating the data as independent would give confidence intervals that are much too narrow. It's like having fewer observations: the "effective sample size" is n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ).

With ρ = 0.9, 2,000 correlated observations carry the information of about 105 independent ones. This is very relevant for network and log data, which are almost never independent: consecutive packets or events of the same session are correlated. An anomaly detector that computes the threshold as 3σ/√n assuming independence will raise many more false alarms than expected. In the extreme case of long-range dependence (covariances decaying so slowly that they are not summable), Var(Sn) grows like n2H with H > ½, and the mean converges slower than 1/√n. Leland et al. (1994) found exactly this self-similar behaviour in measured Ethernet traffic.

Negative correlation does the opposite (ρ = −0.5: n·Var = 1/3, effective sample size 3n): that's the idea behind antithetic variables in Monte Carlo.

No finite variance. Without σ² there is nothing to add. For the Cauchy distribution X̄n has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single observation, for every n: no LLN (E|X| = ∞) and no √n. In my simulation the interquartile range of X̄n was 1.95, 1.94, 2.01, 2.02 for n = 10 … 10,000 (theory: 2), and P(|X̄n| > 1) stayed at 0.5. With infinite variance but finite mean, the LLN still holds but the CLT is replaced by convergence to stable laws with a different scaling n1/α.

Part B

B1Conditional probability

Updating probabilities when we learn something.

If we know that an event B has happened (P(B) > 0), the sample space shrinks to B and the probability of A becomes

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Three rules follow directly from the definition, and they are all that Bayes' theorem needs:

  • Multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)·P(B) = P(B | A)·P(A).
  • Law of total probability: if H1, …, Hk split Ω into disjoint pieces, P(B) = Σi P(B | Hi)·P(Hi).
  • Independence (Homework 6) is exactly the case where conditioning changes nothing: P(A | B) = P(A).

In probability conditioning is a tool for computing things (conditional distributions, conditional expectations, Markov chains, …). In statistics it is the core of the problem itself: a model gives P(data | parameter), i.e. how likely the observations are if the parameter had a certain value, but what we want to know is something about the parameter given the data we observed. The two conditional probabilities are different things and confusing them is a classic mistake (the "prosecutor's fallacy"): P(alert | no attack) can be tiny while P(no attack | alert) is almost 1, as the next example shows.

B2Bayes' theorem: prior, likelihood, posterior

Reversing the conditioning.

Writing P(H ∩ D) in the two ways of the multiplication rule and dividing by P(D):

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D),    P(D) = Σi P(D | Hi) P(Hi)

ComponentSymbolMeaning
PriorP(H) or π(θ)what we believe about the hypothesis / parameter before seeing the data (previous studies, experience, physical constraints, or "no idea" = a flat prior)
LikelihoodP(D | H) or L(θ) = f(data | θ)how well each hypothesis explains the data that were actually observed. As a function of θ it is not a probability distribution (it doesn't sum or integrate to 1 over θ)
EvidenceP(D) = ∫ f(data | θ) π(θ) dθa normalizing constant: it doesn't depend on θ, it just makes the posterior sum to 1
PosteriorP(H | D) or π(θ | data)what we believe after seeing the data: the prior reweighted by how well each value explains the data

Since the evidence is only a constant, the whole theorem is often written as

posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

or, for two hypotheses, in odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio (the likelihood ratio is also called the Bayes factor). On a log scale this becomes a sum: each new piece of evidence adds its log-likelihood ratio to the current log-odds, which is how the spam filter in B5 works.

Why the updating is central

  • It is sequential: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. With conditionally independent data, updating one observation at a time gives exactly the same posterior as using all the data at once, so knowledge can be accumulated as data arrive (alerts, emails, log lines…).
  • The output is a full distribution for the unknown quantity, so we can directly answer questions like "what is the probability that the click rate is above 10%?", which a frequentist confidence interval or p-value doesn't answer (a p-value is P(data at least this extreme | H0), not P(H0 | data)).
  • Prior information enters explicitly and in a transparent way, instead of being ignored or used informally.

B3Example 1: an IDS alert and the base rate

A discrete prior and a likelihood given by the detector's error rates.

An intrusion detection system looks at network connections. Suppose that 1 connection in 1,000 is malicious (prior P(attack) = 0.001), the IDS catches 99% of attacks (sensitivity P(alert | attack) = 0.99) and it raises a false alarm on 1% of the normal connections (P(alert | normal) = 0.01). An alert arrives: what is the probability that it's a real attack?

P(attack | alert) = 0.99·0.001 / (0.99·0.001 + 0.01·0.999) = 0.00099 / 0.01098 ≈ 0.090

Only 9%. The detector looks excellent ("99% accurate") but 91% of its alerts are false alarms, simply because normal connections are 999 times more common. This is the base-rate fallacy: forgetting the prior. Axelsson (2000) showed that this effect is one of the main obstacles for intrusion detection in practice.

Try it

effect of the prior (sensitivity 0.99, false positive rate 0.01)

Updating again. If a second, independent detector (same error rates, errors conditionally independent given the true state) also fires, the 9% becomes the new prior: P(attack | 2 alerts) = 0.908, and after a third alert 0.999. Each alert multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio 0.99/0.01 = 99. The conditional independence assumption is important here: two detectors that look at the same features make correlated mistakes, and then the second alert is worth much less.

What this means: to make alerts trustworthy, improving the sensitivity helps very little; what matters is the false positive rate compared with the base rate (with FPR = 10⁻⁵ the posterior becomes 0.99).

B4Example 2: estimating a click rate (Beta-Binomial)

A continuous parameter, and how much the prior matters.

A company runs a phishing simulation: n = 60 employees get a fake email and k = 9 click the link. Let p be the unknown probability that an employee clicks.

  • Likelihood: the number of clicks is Binomial(n, p), so L(p) ∝ pk(1 − p)n−k. It is maximal at the MLE p̂ = k/n = 0.15.
  • Prior: a Beta(a, b) distribution, π(p) ∝ pa−1(1 − p)b−1, with mean a/(a + b). It works like having already seen a clicks and b non-clicks ("pseudo-observations").
  • Posterior: multiplying, π(p | data) ∝ pa+k−1(1 − p)b+n−k−1, which is again a Beta: Beta(a + k, b + n − k). When prior and posterior are in the same family the prior is called conjugate.

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the data:

E[p | data] = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w·(a/(a + b)) + (1 − w)·(k/n),   w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

so the prior counts as much as a + b observations. I tried four priors (the credible intervals are computed with my own incomplete beta function, checked against scipy to 6 decimals):

  • With the flat prior the posterior is driven by the data; its 95% credible interval [0.082, 0.262] is almost the same as the frequentist Wilson interval [0.081, 0.261]. The interpretation is different though: the credible interval says "given the data, p is in this range with probability 95%".
  • A weak prior from past campaigns (mean 10%, worth 20 observations) pulls the estimate a bit towards 10%; a strong prior (worth 200 observations) dominates the 60 data points: the posterior mean is 0.112 and the interval is narrower.
  • A wrong and strong prior (mean 40%, worth 100 observations) gives a posterior mean of 0.306, twice the observed rate, and P(p > 0.10) = 1.000. The conclusion is completely driven by the prior.
  • The decision "is the click rate above 10%?" changes with the prior: P(p > 0.10 | data) is 0.92, 0.84, 0.71 or 1.00.

B5Example 3: a naive Bayes spam filter

Bayes' theorem with many pieces of evidence, on real data.

I used the SMS Spam Collection (Almeida, Gómez Hidalgo and Yamakami, 2011, UCI Machine Learning Repository): 5,574 real SMS messages labelled as ham (normal) or spam. I trained on a random 75% (3,638 ham, 542 spam) and tested on the other 1,394 messages.

For a message with words w1, …, wm, Bayes' theorem in odds form gives

log [P(spam | w) / P(ham | w)] = log [P(spam)/P(ham)] + Σj log [P(wj | spam) / P(wj | ham)]

  • Prior: P(spam) = 542/4,180 = 0.13, the share of spam in the training data.
  • Likelihood: P(word | class) estimated from word counts. The "naive" assumption is that the words are independent given the class, so the likelihood of the message is the product over its words.
  • Laplace smoothing (add 1 to every count) is itself a prior: a uniform Dirichlet prior on the word probabilities. Without it, a word never seen in spam would get probability 0 and a single word could set P(spam | message) to exactly 0.
Try it
contribution of each word to the log-odds (orange: towards spam, sky blue: towards ham)

Most messages are far from the boundary, so the prior barely matters for them. The "ambiguous" one ("Text me your number to win") gets P(spam) = 0.71 with the learned prior and becomes ham if the prior is moved below about 0.06: with weak evidence the prior decides.

The browser version uses only the words that appear at least 3 times in the training set (2,468 words), the Python version uses all of them, so the numbers can differ a little.

Results on the 1,394 test messages

  • With the prior learned from the data the filter is right 98.6% of the time: it catches 191 of 205 spam messages and wrongly flags 5 of 1,189 normal ones.
  • The prior works like a threshold. Lowering P(spam) to 0.01 removes all false positives (no normal message lost) but lets more spam through (recall 0.91); raising it to 0.9 catches 198 spam but sends 52 normal messages to the spam folder. The data (the likelihood) are the same, only the prior changes, and the conclusions change. For an email filter, losing a real message is worse than seeing a spam, so a low prior is a sensible choice: the prior can encode the cost of the errors, not only beliefs.
  • The most "spammy" words (log-likelihood ratio, among words seen at least 20 times) are claim, prize, 150p, won, tone, guaranteed; the most "hammy" are gt / lt (from the encoded < and >), he, i'll, lor, she, later. The model learns things a human wouldn't write as rules.

Limitation: the independence assumption is clearly false ("claim" and "prize" appear together), so the same evidence is counted several times and the posterior probabilities are much too extreme (often 0.99999 or 0.00001). The classification is still good, because the ranking of messages is mostly right, but the probabilities should not be taken literally.

B6Example 4: a normal prior for a mean

Precision-weighted averaging.

The average response time μ of a web server: from previous weeks I believe μ ~ N(200, 20²) ms. Single requests vary with known σ = 60 ms. Today n = 25 requests have mean x̄ = 236 ms. With a normal prior and a normal likelihood the posterior is normal again, and the precisions (1/variance) add up:

1/τn² = 1/τ0² + n/σ²,    μn = τn² (μ0/τ0² + n·x̄/σ²)

Here: 1/τn² = 1/400 + 25/3600, so the posterior is N(226.5, 10.3²). The prior has weight 0.265 and the data 0.735 (because the data's precision n/σ² = 0.0069 is almost 3 times the prior's 0.0025). Again √n appears: the data's standard error is σ/√n = 12 ms, and as n grows the prior's weight goes to 0.

B7Advantages, limitations, influence on the conclusions

Advantages

  • Uses prior information explicitly (past campaigns, base rates), which is very useful with small samples.
  • Gives direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses (P(p > 0.10 | data), P(attack | alert)).
  • Natural sequential updating: good for streams of alerts, emails, logs.
  • Handles uncertainty in a single coherent framework; extends easily to complex models (hierarchical models, missing data).
  • Priors can regularize (Laplace smoothing avoids zero probabilities) and can encode costs (spam threshold).

Limitations

  • The prior is subjective: different analysts can reach different conclusions with the same data (0.112 vs 0.306 for the click rate).
  • A wrong but confident prior needs a lot of data to be corrected.
  • The base rate must be known: if the real share of attacks is not 0.001, P(attack | alert) is wrong.
  • The likelihood is a model too (independence of words, independence of detectors): if it is wrong, the posterior is wrong, often overconfident.
  • Outside conjugate cases the evidence P(D) is an integral that has to be computed numerically (MCMC, etc.).

Influence on the conclusions. From the examples: the prior matters a lot when the data are few or weak (n = 60 emails; one alert) and becomes irrelevant when the data are many (the wash-out table). When the hypothesis is rare, the prior is the dominant factor even with a very good likelihood (9% after a 99%-sensitive alert). So a good practice is to report the prior, check how much the conclusions change with other reasonable priors (sensitivity analysis, like my four priors in B4), and prefer weakly informative priors unless there is real information.

CConclusions

  • Part A. a.s. convergence is about single paths, convergence in probability about one n at a time, L² about the size of the errors, and convergence in distribution only about the shape of the distribution. a.s. ⇒ P ⇒ D and L² ⇒ P, and the counterexamples (Bernoulli(1/n), the typewriter, n·1{U < 1/n}, −Z) show that the other arrows fail. The LLN holds in L², in probability and a.s.; the CLT only in distribution (Zn and Z2n have correlation 1/√2 for every n).
  • √n appears because for independent (or uncorrelated) observations variances add: Var(Sn) = nσ², so the fluctuations of Sn are of order √n. This gives the σ/√n standard error, the 4×-data-for-half-the-error rule and the scaling of the CLT. With dependence the constant (or even the rate) changes, and without a finite variance the whole √n picture disappears.
  • Part B. Bayes' theorem turns P(data | hypothesis), which a model gives, into P(hypothesis | data), which is what we want, by combining it with a prior: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The examples showed the base-rate effect (an alert from a 99%-sensitive IDS means only 9% probability of attack), conjugate updating (Beta-Binomial, normal-normal) where the posterior is a weighted compromise between prior and data, and a real application (98.6% accuracy on SMS spam) where the prior acts as a decision threshold.
  • The prior has a strong influence with little data and none in the limit, where the posterior concentrates at rate 1/√n, so the two parts of the homework meet in the end.
